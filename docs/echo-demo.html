Echo

Cutting LLM cost without training a router — and what the data actually said when we stopped fooling ourselves.

Nick Meinhold · Robin Langer · Meghana Ganapa · Adarsha Aryal · idea credit: Connor Hood

The problem

Most LLM apps overpay.

A trivial "reverse this string" and a gnarly refactor hit the same expensive endpoint.

The standard fix is a trained router — but that needs labelled data for your domain. That label step is the adoption wall.

What if the cheap model could route itself, with zero training?

The idea

Let the cheap model check itself.

  • Call the cheap model twice, with two different personas.
  • They agree → task was easy. Keep the cheap answer.
  • They disagree → you're on a decision boundary. Escalate.

A difficulty signal, manufactured for free out of the model's own (in)consistency.

Plot twist #1

Our first reasoning numbers
were a lie.

Ported to BBH. Pilot came back flat: Haiku, Sonnet, Echo all ≈ 0.14. Suspiciously equal.

The harness shelled claude -p inside the project dir — loading CLAUDE.md, hooks, task-restore. We were measuring our own agent, not the model.

Raw output literally began: "All 8 tasks restored. Now to your question:" — a 30-second byte dump killed a plausible parser theory. Fix: one flag.

Haiku ties Sonnet, subtask for subtask. MCQ BBH is saturated for 2026 Claude. There is no difficulty gap left to route on.

Difficulty routing's payoff shrinks as the model gap shrinks (Hybrid-LLM, ICLR 2024). Saturation is the zero-gap regime. The original thesis just hit a wall.

Plot twist #2

But the oracle
still won.

Haiku = Sonnet in aggregate — yet echo-oracle beat both (0.899 vs 0.848).

Entirely on one subtask, because Haiku and Sonnet fail on different items. The win isn't a smarter model. It's decorrelated errors.

You don't need the expensive model to be better. You need the two looks to be different.

The pivot · Connor Hood

Route by task TYPE,
not difficulty.

  • Difficulty axis is dead (Haiku = Sonnet). Stop routing on it.
  • Send each task to a hyperspecialist — a small model that owns one domain.
  • Needs Specialist-X > generalist on type X, not smarter overall.

Does it hold up? We checked the literature, then ran it.

Deep research · 27 sources · adversarially verified

Real, but conditional.

  • Math: a 7B specialist beats GPT-4o on MATH specialist wins
  • Code: a 7B specialist loses hard to frontier frontier wins
  • Cheap type-classifiers exist: 1.5B @ 93% / 51ms.

And a warning that cuts to our core: self-consistency fails to capture decorrelated errors — the "popularity trap," models agreeing on identical wrong answers. Only a diversity signal recovers the ceiling.

Bidirectional. Neither model dominates — each covers the other's blind spots. That gap is the routing headroom.

The honest catch

The oracle isn't
deployable.

0.90 needs ground truth — it peeks at the answer. The real question: can a cheap router capture that 16-point gap?

And the popularity trap warns us: self-consistency won't. Agreement and correctness decouple exactly where the errors are decorrelated. The lever is a diversity signal, not a better judge.

We already measured this tax in BBH: judge 0.869 vs oracle 0.899. Closing that gap is the next experiment.
